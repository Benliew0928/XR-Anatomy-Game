## Executive summary

The proposal is the foundation for FYP 1 and FYP 2, not a one-off document. Keep the project feasible for an individual student, identify a small and defensible contribution over existing work, and make objectives measurable. The proposal should already contain a strong abstract, a focused literature review with evidence, a comparison table, a planned methodology, and a basic prototype direction.

The presenter will assess the **oral presentation**, not the written report. The report must be handled according to the instructions from the student's class lecturer/supervisor. Presentation feedback must be recorded and incorporated into FYP 1.

## Proposal report guidance

### Abstract

The abstract should be a **single paragraph on one page**, functioning as a concise synopsis of the entire project. It should cover these elements in this order:

1. **Background**
2. **Motivation / problem statement**
3. **Main objective**
4. **Contribution**
5. **Methodology**
6. **Expected results**

The presenter described motivation and the problem statement as closely linked: a project begins because a real problem provides the motivation. The abstract matters greatly because it is the first section likely to be read by a moderator, supervisor, or future employer. It should be clear enough to make the reader want to continue.

The guideline visible on screen also stated:

- **Areas of Study:** minimum 1 and maximum 2
- **Keywords:** minimum 5 and maximum 10

### Literature review

For a **development-based project**, prepare at least:

- **3 related/existing systems, applications, websites, or similar work**
- **3 relevant journal articles**; the presenter recommended using journals at this stage

Evidence to include:

- For an existing system or application, include a **screenshot of its UI/interface**.
- For each journal article, include a **screenshot of the first page**, showing information such as the author, title, and abstract.
- Include diagrams where useful, but cite them correctly.
- Use **IEEE citation format**.

### Comparison table and contribution

Add a **comparison table at the end of the literature review chapter**. Compare relevant features, parameters, or variables across the existing systems and/or related work.

The comparison is not intended only to show that other work exists. It must help identify limitations that the proposed system will address:

1. Examine existing apps, systems, or websites.
2. Identify their weaknesses, gaps, or missing elements.
3. Select **one or two realistic gaps** that the proposed project can improve.
4. State those improvements as the project's contribution.

Keep the scope modest. An individual student should not attempt to reproduce or outperform a product made by a large development team. Even a small, well-justified improvement can be a valid contribution.

### Methodology and system design

Explain:

- The software, technologies, or tools planned for the project
- How the project/system will be implemented
- The proposed design or process flow

Use a **flowchart** to show the implementation or project flow when appropriate.

## Objectives: use SMART objectives

Use **two or three objectives only**. Do not create a long list. Every objective should be SMART:

- **Specific**
- **Measurable**
- **Achievable**
- **Relevant**
- **Time-bound**

Use clear action verbs, for example:

- “To implement …”
- “To evaluate …”
- “To study …”

Be cautious with broad claims such as “to increase awareness” or “to improve productivity.” They are acceptable only if there is a valid plan to measure and prove them, for example through a questionnaire, usability test, or another defined evaluation method.

## Recommended proposal structure

1. **Abstract** — one-page, one-paragraph synopsis containing the six required elements.
2. **Chapter 1: Background / introduction** — explain the context, problem, motivation, and project direction.
3. **Chapter 2: Literature review** — existing systems, relevant journals, screenshots, diagrams with IEEE citations, comparison table, and identified gaps.
4. **Objectives and contribution** — two or three SMART objectives plus the specific improvement the system will offer.
5. **Methodology / design** — software/tools, implementation approach, and flowchart.
6. **Expected results** — what the proposed system/prototype is expected to achieve and how the result will be demonstrated or evaluated.

## Pre-presentation checklist

- [ ] I have read the official IIPSPW guidelines and schedule PDF.
- [ ] My abstract is one paragraph, one page, and includes all six required elements.
- [ ] I have selected 1–2 areas of study and 5–10 keywords.
- [ ] I have only 2–3 SMART, measurable objectives.
- [ ] I have reviewed 3 existing systems/apps/websites and captured their UI screenshots.
- [ ] I have selected 3 relevant journal articles and captured their first pages.
- [ ] I have included an IEEE-cited literature review and cited any diagrams.
- [ ] I have a comparison table that identifies realistic gaps in existing solutions.
- [ ] My contribution directly addresses one or two of those gaps.
- [ ] My scope is achievable for one student.
- [ ] I have documented tools/software, methodology, and a project/system flowchart.
- [ ] I know how the proposed outcome will be evaluated.
- [ ] I am ready to record feedback during the oral presentation and apply it in FYP 1.

## Final takeaway

A strong proposal is specific, evidence-based, and achievable. Build the project around a real, clearly explained problem; show what existing solutions are missing; propose a small but meaningful contribution; and make sure the objectives and evaluation method prove what the project claims to achieve.
